# Helms Row

## Overview

The Helms row is an elbow-flared, upper-back-biased dumbbell row typically performed chest-supported on an incline bench, emphasizing scapular retraction and rear-delt/upper-back stimulus with a stretch at the bottom.

## Muscles Worked

### Primary

- Rhomboids and middle trapezius
- Rear deltoids
- Latissimus dorsi (still involved)

### Secondary

- Elbow flexors
- Posterior shoulder musculature

### Stabilizers

- Scapular stabilizers against the bench

## Equipment

- Adjustable incline bench
- Pair of dumbbells

## Difficulty

intermediate

## Execution

1. Set a moderate incline; lie chest-down holding dumbbells.
2. Let arms hang with a stretch; elbows relatively flared versus a hip-oriented row.
3. Row by retracting scapulae and pulling elbows out/back toward the upper torso.
4. Squeeze upper back; lower into the stretch.
5. Keep chest on the pad.

## Coaching Cues

- "Chest on the pad"
- "Flare and retract"
- "Stretch the upper back"
- "Squeeze at the top"

## Common Mistakes

- Turning into a hip-driven row
- Peeling off the pad
- Using too much load and losing stretch
- Shrugging into the neck

## Breathing

Inhale to prepare or during the eccentric lengthening phase. Exhale during the concentric pull (or near peak contraction). Brace the trunk; avoid breath-holding that causes dizziness on high-rep sets.

## Range of Motion

Deep stretch at the bottom to strong upper-back contraction.

## Progressions

- Load
- Tempo stretches
- Wide-grip cable row pairings

## Regressions

- Chest-supported machine row
- Face-pull
- Lighter standard DB row

## Programming

### Sets

- 3–4 sets

### Reps

- 10–15

### Intensity

- RIR 1–3

### Rest

- 60–90 seconds

### Frequency

- 1–3x weekly

### Best Goals

#### Hypertrophy

Upper-back and rear-delt biased row variation.

#### Strength

Not a primary strength lift.

#### Endurance

Higher-rep suitable.

#### Fat Loss

Accessory volume tool.

## Contraindications

May be inappropriate with symptomatic shoulder impingement, acute elbow tendinopathy, recent lat or biceps strain, uncontrolled low-back pain during hinged positions, or inability to control scapular motion. This is training guidance, not medical diagnosis.
