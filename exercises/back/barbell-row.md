# Barbell Row

## Overview

The barbell row is a bent-over horizontal pulling exercise that loads the upper and mid-back, lats, and elbow flexors while demanding isometric trunk and hip stability. It is a primary free-weight row for building pulling strength and back hypertrophy and pairs well with vertical pulls such as pulldowns or pull-ups.

## Primary Muscles

- Latissimus dorsi
- Rhomboids and middle trapezius
- Posterior shoulder complex contributions based on elbow path

## Secondary Muscles

- Biceps brachii and other elbow flexors
- Rear delts
- Spinal erectors (isometric support)
- Abdominals / trunk stabilizers
- Glutes and hamstrings (isometric hip hinge support)

## Equipment

- Barbell
- Optional: lifting straps for high-rep sets when grip limits back loading
- Optional: rack setup for consistent starting height

## Difficulty

intermediate

## Benefits

- Compound horizontal-pull overload with measurable load progression
- Trains scapular retraction and elbow-driven pulling together
- Builds trunk stability under load when hinged position is maintained
- Complements vertical pulling for balanced back development

## Execution

1. Stand with feet roughly hip-to-shoulder width. Hinge at the hips to a torso angle that can be maintained (commonly ~15–45° above parallel, individualized).
2. Grip the bar just outside the legs. Brace the trunk; keep a neutral spine and soft knees.
3. Pull the bar toward the lower chest/upper abdomen by driving the elbows back while retracting the scapulae.
4. Pause briefly without turning the lift into a shrug-only or lumbar-extension finish.
5. Lower the bar under control toward full arm extension without losing the hinge position; repeat.

## Common Mistakes

- Standing too upright and converting the row into a partial shrug/curl
- Using hip drive and lumbar extension as the primary movers
- Rounding the thoracic/lumbar spine under fatigue
- Cutting ROM and never reaching a true stretch or contraction
- Excessively wide elbows that irritate the shoulders without improving back tension

## Coaching Cues

- “Hips back, spine long”
- “Pull elbows to the hips/ribs”
- “Squeeze the shoulder blades, don’t yank the bar”
- “Lower with control; stay hinged”

## Programming

Use as a primary horizontal pull on back, upper, or pull days. Strength-focused sets work well in lower-to-moderate reps with longer rests. Hypertrophy-focused sets benefit from strict torso position, controlled eccentrics, and 0–3 RIR. If lower-back fatigue limits quality, substitute chest-supported rows for some volume.

## Sets

- Strength focus: 3–5 sets
- Hypertrophy focus: 3–4 sets

## Reps

- Strength focus: 4–6 reps
- Hypertrophy focus: 6–12 reps

## Tempo

- 2-0-1-0 to 3-1-1-0

## Rest Time

- Strength focus: 2–3 minutes
- Hypertrophy focus: 90–180 seconds

## Variations

- Pendlay row
- Yates row (more upright torso)
- Underhand barbell row
- Chest-supported barbell or dumbbell row

## Regression

- Chest-supported row
- Seated cable row
- Dumbbell single-arm row

## Progression

- Progressive load increases
- Paused rows at peak contraction
- Slow eccentrics
- Pendlay resets for strength emphasis

## Contraindications

May be inappropriate during symptomatic low-back pain aggravated by hinging, recent lumbar injury, uncontrolled high blood pressure responses to heavy straining, or active biceps/elbow tendinopathy worsened by gripping and pulling. This is training guidance, not medical diagnosis.

## Science Summary

Horizontal rowing exercises produce substantial activation of the latissimus dorsi and scapular retractors and are standard components of evidence-based resistance programs for upper-body pulling strength and hypertrophy. Free-weight bent-over rows add high trunk stability demand compared with supported machines, which can be beneficial for transfer but may limit load if spinal endurance fails first. Hand spacing and torso angle alter regional emphasis; no single row style is universally superior. Weekly pulling volume, progressive overload, and controlled technique predict adaptation better than attachment novelty.
