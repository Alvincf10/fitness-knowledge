# Barbell Bench Press

## Overview

The barbell bench press is a horizontal pressing exercise that loads the pectoralis major with substantial contribution from the triceps brachii and anterior deltoids. It is widely used for upper-body strength and hypertrophy because it allows progressive overload with measurable load increments and has extensive support in resistance-training research and professional guidelines.

## Primary Muscles

- Pectoralis major (sternal and clavicular heads)

## Secondary Muscles

- Triceps brachii
- Anterior deltoids
- Serratus anterior (stabilization)
- Scapular retractors (stabilization)

## Equipment

- Barbell
- Flat bench
- Power rack or dedicated bench press station
- Safety pins or spotter recommended for near-maximal loads

## Difficulty

intermediate

## Benefits

- Efficient compound stimulus for chest, triceps, and front delts
- High capacity for progressive overload and strength tracking
- Strong transfer to horizontal pressing performance
- Well studied in hypertrophy and strength literature as a primary free-weight press

## Execution

1. Lie on a flat bench with eyes roughly under the bar. Plant feet firmly. Retract and depress the scapulae; maintain a stable upper-back position.
2. Grip the bar evenly (commonly slightly wider than shoulder width). Unrack with locked elbows and bring the bar over the mid-chest/shoulder line.
3. Lower the bar under control to the mid-to-lower chest while elbows track at a moderate angle (typically ~45–70° from the torso, individualized to shoulder comfort).
4. Press the bar upward and slightly back toward the start position until elbows are extended without losing scapular stability.
5. Re-rack only when the bar is stable over the supports.

## Common Mistakes

- Excessive elbow flare that increases anterior shoulder stress
- Bouncing the bar off the chest and losing eccentric control
- Losing scapular retraction and turning the press into a shoulder-dominant pattern
- Uncontrolled arch that lifts the hips off the bench
- Incomplete range that chronically shortens the pressing ROM without a programming reason

## Coaching Cues

- “Pins back and down; chest up”
- “Break the bar and lower to the chest”
- “Drive feet into the floor as you press”
- “Finish over the shoulders, not over the face”

## Programming

Use as a primary horizontal press on upper or push days. For strength, prioritize lower-rep heavy sets with longer rests. For hypertrophy, use moderate loads with controlled eccentrics and proximity to failure managed via RIR. Pair with incline pressing or fly/cable work when greater clavicular-chest or stretch-biased volume is desired.

## Sets

- Strength focus: 3–5 sets
- Hypertrophy focus: 3–4 sets

## Reps

- Strength focus: 3–6 reps
- Hypertrophy focus: 6–12 reps

## Tempo

- Hypertrophy: 2-0-1-0 to 3-1-1-0
- Strength: controlled eccentric, intentional concentric (often 2-0-X-0)

## Rest Time

- Strength focus: 2–5 minutes
- Hypertrophy focus: 90–180 seconds

## Variations

- Pause bench press
- Close-grip bench press
- Incline barbell bench press
- Dumbbell bench press

## Regression

- Dumbbell bench press
- Machine chest press
- Push-up

## Progression

- Paused heavy singles/doubles
- Tempo eccentrics
- Competition-style pause bench
- Progressive overload via load, reps, or set volume

## Contraindications

May be inappropriate during symptomatic anterior shoulder irritation, recent pectoralis or triceps strain, uncontrolled wrist/elbow pain, or when a safe rack/spotting setup is unavailable. This is training guidance, not medical diagnosis.

## Science Summary

Horizontal pressing is a core pattern in resistance-training recommendations for upper-body strength and muscle development. EMG and training studies consistently show high pectoralis major involvement in the flat bench press, with meaningful triceps and anterior deltoid contribution. Hypertrophy outcomes depend more on sufficient weekly volume, progressive overload, and proximity to failure than on any single exercise, but the bench press remains an effective primary loader because external resistance can be progressed precisely. Grip width and elbow angle alter joint stress and regional emphasis; individual anthropometry should guide technique within a controlled ROM.
