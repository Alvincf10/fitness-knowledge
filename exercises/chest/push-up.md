# Push-Up

## Overview

The push-up is a closed-chain horizontal pressing exercise that loads the pectoralis major, triceps, and anterior deltoids while requiring trunk stability. It is scalable from wall or knee variations to advanced loaded or deficit versions, making it suitable for beginners and as accessory or home-training work for trained individuals.

## Primary Muscles

- Pectoralis major

## Secondary Muscles

- Triceps brachii
- Anterior deltoids
- Serratus anterior
- Abdominals and other trunk stabilizers

## Equipment

- Bodyweight
- Optional: parallettes, handles, weight vest, or elevated surfaces for regressions/progressions

## Difficulty

beginner

## Benefits

- Accessible horizontal press with minimal equipment
- Trains pressing strength and hypertrophy potential when progressed sufficiently
- Concurrent trunk stability demand compared with supported bench pressing
- Easy to modify difficulty via leverage, ROM, tempo, or external load

## Execution

1. Place hands roughly under or slightly outside the shoulders. Extend the body into a plank: ribs down, glutes lightly engaged, head neutral.
2. Lower the chest toward the floor by bending the elbows while maintaining a rigid trunk.
3. Descend until the chest approaches the floor or reaches the deepest controllable ROM without losing plank alignment.
4. Press through the hands to full elbow extension while keeping scapulae moving naturally (avoid collapsing or extreme winging).
5. Reset tension and repeat.

## Common Mistakes

- Sagging hips or piking the pelvis, reducing transfer to the chest press pattern
- Cutting depth chronically without progression intent
- Flaring elbows hard to 90° and irritating the shoulders
- Leading with the head or cervical hyperextension
- Losing scapular control and dumping into the shoulders at the bottom

## Coaching Cues

- “Body in one line from head to heels”
- “Screw hands into the floor”
- “Chest to the floor, not hips first”
- “Push the floor away”

## Programming

Use as a primary press for beginners/home trainees or as accessory volume after heavier bench work. For hypertrophy, train near technical failure with progressions that keep reps in a productive range (roughly 6–15+ depending on variation). Increase difficulty before chasing very high-rep sets that become trunk-limited or form-limited.

## Sets

- 2–4 sets

## Reps

- Beginners: 5–12 controlled reps per set (use regressions as needed)
- Intermediate+: 6–15 reps on a challenging variation

## Tempo

- 2-0-1-0 to 3-1-1-0

## Rest Time

- 60–120 seconds
- Up to 180 seconds for hard weighted or deficit variations

## Variations

- Knee push-up
- Elevated-hand push-up
- Deficit push-up
- Tempo push-up
- Weighted vest push-up
- Diamond or close-grip push-up

## Regression

- Wall push-up
- Incline push-up (hands elevated)
- Knee push-up

## Progression

- Deficit push-up
- Feet-elevated push-up
- Weighted push-up
- Paused or slow-eccentric push-up
- Ring or unstable-surface push-up (advanced stability demand)

## Contraindications

May be inappropriate with acute wrist pain, symptomatic anterior shoulder irritation, recent chest/shoulder strain, or inability to maintain plank alignment without pain. Modify hand elevation or use handles if wrist extension is poorly tolerated. This is training guidance, not medical diagnosis.

## Science Summary

Push-ups produce substantial pectoralis major and triceps activation and can improve upper-body strength and muscle thickness when loading is progressed. Comparative work shows that sufficiently hard push-up variations can approximate aspects of bench-press muscle recruitment, though absolute external load and overload precision remain higher with free weights. Because bodyweight difficulty is leverage-dependent, programming should prioritize harder variations or added load once high-rep sets no longer provide progressive stimulus. Core involvement is higher than in bench pressing due to the unsupported plank position.
