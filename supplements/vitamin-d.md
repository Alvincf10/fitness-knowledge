# Vitamin D

## Overview

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble nutrient involved in bone health, immune function, and musculoskeletal physiology. Supplementation is most justified when status is low due to limited sun exposure, diet, or other risk factors—not as a universal performance enhancer.

## What It Is

Vitamin D refers mainly to D3 (cholecalciferol) and D2 (ergocalciferol). Status is commonly assessed via serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D in clinical settings.

## Benefits

- Correcting deficiency supports bone and musculoskeletal health
- May help athletes with low baseline status restore normal function
- Performance benefits are inconsistent when status is already sufficient
- Not a primary hypertrophy supplement

## Who Should Take It

People with limited sun exposure, darker skin in low-UV environments, indoor athletes, or known low status under clinical guidance. Routine high-dose use without assessing need is unnecessary for many.

## Recommended Dose

- Common maintenance intakes often fall near dietary reference ranges (for example on the order of 600–2000 IU/day depending on guidance and context)
- Higher repletion doses should be clinician-directed based on labs
- Avoid megadoses without medical supervision

## Timing

Daily with a meal containing fat is practical. Workout timing is irrelevant for primary outcomes.

## Side Effects

- Excess intake can cause hypercalcemia and toxicity
- Fat-soluble accumulation makes unsupervised megadosing risky

## Interactions

- Works with calcium and bone-health nutrition patterns
- May interact with certain medications; clinical advice applies when relevant
- Do not stack aggressive unsupervised fat-soluble vitamin megadoses

## Evidence Summary

Evidence is strong that correcting deficiency matters for health. Meta-analyses on vitamin D and athletic performance show mixed results, with clearer rationale for repletion than for boosting already sufficient athletes. Treat as a status-dependent nutrient, not a pre-workout ergogenic.
