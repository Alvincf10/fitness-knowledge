# Micronutrients

## Overview

Micronutrients are vitamins and minerals required in small amounts for energy metabolism, bone health, oxygen transport, immune function, and recovery. Deficiencies can impair training; megadosing extras rarely boost performance in already sufficient athletes.

## Definition

Micronutrients include vitamins (water- and fat-soluble) and minerals (such as iron, calcium, magnesium, zinc). Needs are met primarily through dietary patterns, with targeted supplementation when intake or status is inadequate.

## Daily Recommendation

- Follow age/sex dietary reference patterns through food variety first
- Prioritize iron, calcium, vitamin D, and related nutrients in at-risk groups
- Use labs and clinical guidance for suspected deficiency rather than blanket megadoses
- Multivitamins are optional insurance, not a substitute for diet quality

## Benefits

- Supports normal physiological function required for training adaptations
- Reduces risk of deficiency-related fatigue and impaired recovery in at-risk athletes
- Improves long-term health alongside performance nutrition

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming more vitamins equal more muscle
- Ignoring low energy availability while buying supplements
- Restrictive cutting diets that remove micronutrient-dense foods
- Self-prescribing high-dose fat-soluble vitamins

## Example Foods

- Fruits and vegetables for diverse vitamins
- Dairy or fortified alternatives for calcium
- Lean meats, fish, legumes for iron and zinc
- Fatty fish, eggs, fortified foods for vitamin D support

## Practical Tips

- Build plates with produce plus protein at most meals
- During cuts, keep micronutrient-dense foods while trimming calorie-dense extras
- Consider vitamin D status if indoor lifestyle and low sun exposure
- Fix energy and protein before stacking specialty micronutrient products

## Evidence Summary

Sports-nutrition position stands emphasize food-first micronutrient adequacy. Performance benefits from supplementation are clearest when correcting deficiency (for example iron or vitamin D in low-status athletes), not when status is already sufficient.
