# Alcohol

## Overview

Alcohol is an energy-containing compound that can impair recovery, sleep quality, and training adaptations when intake is high or poorly timed. Occasional low intake may fit some lifestyles, but it is not performance-enhancing.

## Definition

Ethanol provides calories (~7 kcal/g) without meaningful sports-nutrition benefit. It affects protein synthesis signaling, hydration status, and sleep architecture in dose-dependent ways.

## Daily Recommendation

- For performance-focused phases, minimize or avoid alcohol near hard training and recovery nights
- If consumed, account for its calories within energy goals
- Avoid binge patterns that disrupt sleep and adherence
- Follow public-health low-risk guidelines; zero is optimal for many training blocks

## Benefits

- No unique hypertrophy or strength benefit
- Possible social adherence value for some people when kept minimal
- Performance nutrition prioritizes reduction rather than inclusion

## Common Mistakes

- Ignoring alcohol calories during a cut
- Heavy drinking after hard training sessions
- Using alcohol for “recovery” when it worsens sleep
- Assuming beer carbs usefully replace planned carbohydrate fueling

## Example Foods

- Not applicable as a recommended fuel source
- If included, prefer planned modest amounts rather than untracked binge drinking
- Non-alcoholic alternatives for social settings during hard phases

## Practical Tips

- Keep hard training days and key sleep nights alcohol-free during cuts or strength peaks
- Log drinks if body composition is tightly managed
- Rehydrate and return to protein-forward meals after any intake
- Evaluate whether alcohol is blocking fat-loss adherence

## Evidence Summary

Research indicates alcohol can reduce rates of muscle protein synthesis and impair sleep at relevant doses, with clearer harm at binge levels. Sports-nutrition practice treats alcohol as optional and often counterproductive during optimization phases. Evidence is moderate-to-strong for impairment risk, not for benefit.
