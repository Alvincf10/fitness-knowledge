# Fat

## Overview

Dietary fat supplies energy, supports absorption of fat-soluble vitamins, and provides essential fatty acids. For lifters, fat is rarely the main driver of hypertrophy, but chronically very low intakes can impair diet quality and health.

## Definition

Dietary fats include triglycerides composed of saturated, monounsaturated, and polyunsaturated fatty acids. Essential fatty acids (including omega-3 and omega-6 families) must come from the diet.

## Daily Recommendation

- Common sports-nutrition practice keeps fat high enough for health—often roughly 20–35% of calories, individualized
- Avoid extreme ultra-low-fat diets unless clinically supervised
- Emphasize unsaturated sources while managing total calories

## Benefits

- Provides dense energy when calorie needs are high
- Supports fat-soluble vitamin absorption (A, D, E, K)
- Supplies essential fatty acids
- Improves meal satisfaction for many people

## Common Mistakes

- Cutting fat to near-zero during a cut
- Assuming high fat automatically improves hormones regardless of energy balance and body fat
- Ignoring calorie density of oils and nuts during fat loss
- Relying on supplements instead of fixing overall fat quality

## Example Foods

- Olive oil, avocado, nuts, seeds
- Fatty fish
- Eggs and dairy fats in context of total diet
- Nut butters measured for calorie control

## Practical Tips

- Set protein first, fuel training with carbs as needed, then fill remaining calories with fats
- Prefer whole-food fat sources most of the time
- Include fatty fish or consider omega-3 planning if intake is low
- Measure calorie-dense fats during aggressive cuts

## Evidence Summary

Dietary guidelines and ACSM sports-nutrition positions support including adequate fat for health while matching energy to goals. Hypertrophy outcomes depend more on protein, progressive training, and total energy than on high fat per se.
